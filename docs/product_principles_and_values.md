# RavenHill — Product Principles & Values

*Last updated: March 28, 2026.*

---

## Core Belief

Work should be doing your job — not coordinating about doing your job. Every meeting, status check, follow-up email, and "quick question" that exists only to move information from one person to another is overhead that AI agents can eliminate.

The end state: each employee focuses entirely on their task-specific work. Communication, reporting, and information retrieval happen automatically through agents. No one waits on information. No one gets interrupted for a status update. Everyone is maximally productive.

We get there gradually — not all at once.

---

## Principles

### 1. The agent works for the employee, not for management.

This is the line that separates RavenHill from surveillance. Your agent represents *you*. It shares information on *your* terms. It protects your time by handling interruptions so you can focus. It is a shield, not a monitor.

The cautionary tale: early internet-era companies created screen-monitoring software that took periodic screenshots of employee desktops to ensure productivity. Employees pushed back, trust eroded, and every one of those companies eventually died. The failure was directionality — those tools reported upward. RavenHill's agents report outward, on behalf of the employee, with the employee in control.

Business requires trust between employer and employee. We will not build anything that breaks that trust.

### 2. Information flows because sharing becomes unnecessary, not because privacy is removed.

The goal is to make information hoarding structurally pointless. In some companies, individuals make themselves invaluable by acting as gatekeepers for important or exclusive information. This is a symptom of corporate bureaucracy, and it's bad for the organization. RavenHill dissolves this problem without confrontation — the bottleneck stops mattering because information routes around it naturally. Nobody gets called out. No reorg required.

### 3. Default to "not ready."

Work products are private until the employee says otherwise. Drafts are never shared automatically. If someone's agent requests a document that isn't marked ready, the response is honest about status without exposing the work: "They're working on it, deadline is Thursday."

The readiness signal must be a solved problem. Edge cases matter — deadlines, urgent requests, context-dependent situations. Pattern-matching on editing behavior alone is not good enough. The agent needs to understand context: who's asking, why, and what the deadline looks like.

### 4. The agent is your gatekeeper.

When your boss or coworker needs something from you — their agent talks to your agent — not to you. Your agent handles the back-and-forth: what's ready, what's in progress, when things will be done. You never get tapped on the shoulder. You never context-switch to answer a question your agent already knows the answer to.

This isn't just efficiency — it prevents micromanagement. The agent provides enough visibility into timing and status that the requesting party gets what they actually need without interrupting the person doing the work.

### 5. Proactive, not prescriptive.

The agent surfaces information and alerts. It does not make strategic recommendations or personnel judgments. The human does the thinking. The agent makes sure the human has everything they need to think well.

### 6. Discovery over permissions.

Early interviews suggest that most documents in an organization are already shared with the people who need them (OneDrive folders, shared drives, etc.). If that holds broadly, the bigger pain may not be "I can't access this file" but "I didn't know this file existed." That would make RavenHill's primary document function discovery — surfacing things you already have access to but wouldn't think to look for — with permission requests as the exception for people outside the normal access group. Further interviews are needed to validate this and related pain points.

### 7. Boil the frog.

This is a large change to how companies operate. You don't walk in and say "we're replacing how your organization communicates." You start with one narrow pain point — maybe it's eliminating status update meetings — and expand once people can't imagine going back. Each new layer feels like a small quality-of-life improvement, not a transformation. One day the entire coordination layer has been quietly absorbed, and nobody remembers the moment it happened.

### 8. Institutional memory belongs to the company.

When an employee leaves, their agent's accumulated knowledge stays. A new hire inherits it and ramps up in days instead of months. This is not surveillance of the departing employee — it's preservation of organizational knowledge that would otherwise walk out the door.

---

## What We Will Not Build

- Anything that monitors employee behavior and reports it upward
- Anything that shares work without the employee's explicit readiness signal
- Anything that makes strategic or personnel recommendations
- Anything that replaces human judgment — only the coordination layer around it
